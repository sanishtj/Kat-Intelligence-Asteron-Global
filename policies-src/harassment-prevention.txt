TITLE: Workplace Harassment Prevention Policy
SHORT: workplace-harassment-prevention
VERSION: Version 4.1, effective 1 February 2026
OWNER: People and Culture
INTRO: Everyone at Asteron Global is entitled to a workplace free from harassment, bullying and intimidation, in the office, on company systems and at work-related events. This policy explains what harassment is, how to report it, and how reports are handled.

## 1. Purpose
This policy prevents harassment at work and sets out how Asteron Global responds when it is reported. It supports the Code of Conduct and Ethics and applies alongside any stronger protections provided by local law, which prevail where they differ.

## 2. Who is covered
This policy protects and applies to all employees, interns, contractors and agency workers, at every level, and covers behaviour by colleagues, managers, customers, suppliers and visitors. It applies in offices, factories and warehouses, on business travel, at work social events, and in work-related communications on any platform, including messaging apps and social media.

## 3. What harassment is
Harassment is unwanted conduct that violates a person's dignity or creates an intimidating, hostile, degrading or offensive environment for them. It can be a single serious incident or a pattern of smaller acts. Examples include:
- Repeated unwanted messages, calls or approaches, in person or online, after the person has shown they are not welcome.
- Comments, jokes or remarks about a person's appearance, body, personal life or characteristics.
- Sharing images, memes or messages of a sexual or demeaning nature in work channels or groups.
- Deliberately excluding someone from meetings or information they need, mocking them, or spreading rumours about them.
- Unwanted physical contact, leering, or blocking someone's movement.
Conduct does not stop being harassment because the person responsible describes it as a joke, a compliment or friendly behaviour. What matters is the effect on the person receiving it and whether a reasonable person would find it unwanted.

## 4. Sexual harassment
Sexual harassment includes unwelcome sexual advances, requests for sexual favours, suggestive comments or messages, questions about a person's private life, and displaying sexual material. Repeatedly messaging a colleague about their appearance, asking them out after they have declined, or sending personal messages late at night that are unrelated to work can all amount to sexual harassment, whether or not the sender intended harm.

## 5. Bullying and intimidation
Bullying includes shouting at or humiliating a colleague, setting impossible deadlines to set someone up to fail, taking credit for another person's work, and threatening someone's job or standing without a legitimate business reason. Firm, fair management, including honest feedback about performance delivered respectfully, is not bullying.

## 6. Your responsibilities
Every employee must treat colleagues with respect and must not engage in, encourage or ignore harassment. If you witness harassment, you can support the person affected and, with their knowledge where possible, report what you saw. Managers who receive a report or observe harassment must act on it the same day by contacting People and Culture; a manager who ignores a report is in violation of this policy.

## 7. How to report
You can report harassment to your manager, to People and Culture, or through the Speak-Up channel described in the Speak-Up and Whistleblowing Policy. You may report anonymously. You do not need proof before reporting; you need only describe what happened, when, and who was involved or present. Reports can also be made by a witness on behalf of the person affected.

## 8. How reports are handled
Reports are acknowledged within two business days and assessed promptly. Where an investigation is needed, it is carried out by a trained investigator who is not connected to the people involved. Both the person reporting and the person accused are treated fairly, are told the outcome in appropriate terms, and are protected while the matter is open. Interim steps, such as separating reporting lines or work locations, may be taken to keep everyone safe and are not a judgement on the outcome.

## 9. No retaliation
Retaliation against anyone who reports harassment, supports a report or takes part in an investigation is itself a serious violation and will be treated as a separate disciplinary matter. If you believe you are experiencing retaliation, report it immediately through any channel in section 7.

## 10. Consequences
Substantiated harassment may result in disciplinary action up to and including dismissal, proportionate to the seriousness and persistence of the behaviour. Malicious reports made in bad faith are also a violation; a report that is not substantiated is not, by itself, treated as made in bad faith.

## 11. Worked examples
Example. A team member repeatedly sends a colleague personal messages late at night commenting on her appearance and asking her out. She has replied once that she wants the messages to stop, but they continue. This is the pattern described in sections 3 and 4: repeated unwanted messages after the person has shown they are not welcome. She can report it to People and Culture or through the Speak-Up channel, and she does not need to collect further proof before doing so.
Example. In team meetings, a manager regularly imitates an employee's accent and invites others to laugh. Singling out a colleague's characteristics for mockery is harassment under section 3, even when presented as humour. A witness may report it under section 6, and the manager's own manager must act on the report the same day.
Example. Two colleagues who are friends exchange teasing messages that both welcome and can stop at any time. Welcomed behaviour between equals, which either person can end, is not harassment. If one of them becomes uncomfortable, the behaviour must stop as soon as that is known; continuing it would bring it within section 3.
